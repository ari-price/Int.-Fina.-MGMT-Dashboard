Analysis: The 13.75% interest rate found in Brazil is one of the highest ones in Latin America according to the results from Trading Economics, which in consequence makes the fixed income market more attractive because of the high return and could act as an incentive to increase the value of the Brazilian currency.

But, also makes lending interests high as well, so the corporate and particular investment could be affected negatively.

As the forecast of the SELIC shows with anticipation its planned decrease, this means that in the short term, the corporate investment could improve slightly (The amount is not that significant yet) for borrowing at lower interest rates. The Brazilian currency (BRL) then, would be pressured if the interest rate for fixed income investors is not as high as previously, but again, the difference is not that significant yet. This explains why the real interest rate remains almost unchanged from December 2026 to June 2027.

Risk Matrix

Inflation Shocks

Analysis: Although Month-on- month inflation had a contraction (-0.32% in August 2026) and year-on-year CPI stood at 4.22%, inflation is forecasted to reaccelerate to 4.9% by December 2026, which exceeds the Central Bank’s upper target range (4.5%).

FX Pass-Through: With the exchange rate trading around 5.20 BRL/USD and marginal real depreciation, there is a risk of import price pass-through (FX pass-through) into domestic prices, particularly across rigid basket components such as Health (5.84%) and Housing (4.90%).

Monetary Tightening: While the government is expected to slowly lower its main interest rate (the Selic rate) from 13.75% down to 13.50% by December 2026 and 12.00% in 2027, the actual cost of borrowing after inflation will still stay very high (8.5% – 9.5%). This is much higher than the 5.0% balanced rate estimated by the IMF. Keeping interest rates this high will make borrowing expensive, which will slow down the economy's recovery

Political Instability

Government Spending and Debt Problems: The country’s financial health is the biggest risk at home. Total government debt is very high, sitting at 78.9% – 81.5% of the country's total economic output, and it is growing by 5.27% every year. Because the government is projected to spend more than it earns in 2026 (a shortage of -0.42%), its long-term financial future is getting worse.

High Cost of Government Borrowing: Financial markets see the country as risky and are demanding high interest rates to lend it money. Investors want higher returns for locking their money away longer, with rates rising from 13.58% for 1-year bonds up to 14.20% for 10-year bonds, alongside a high insurance cost (203.14 bps) to protect against government default.

Political Risk and Unpredictable Policies: Political tension (0.031 score) shows that arguments in Congress over the budget and upcoming elections are a big problem. These events could force the government to spend even more money, making it break its own rules about responsible spending.

Regulatory Changes

Fiscal Transition and Sectoral Incentives: Changes in taxes and government subsidies may affect costs for different industries and services.

Environmental Framework and ESG Compliance: Brazil has an LSEG Sovereign ESG Global Score of 63.02 and a Sovereign Sustainable GDP Gap of 2.29%. Brazil is facing stricter environmental rules. Requirements to reduce emissions and track supply chains could increase operating costs (OPEX) and create risks, especially for agribusiness and extractive industries.

Commodity Price Fluctuations

Vulnerability to Export Prices: Brazil depends heavily on exports and on the commodity cycle. Its trade balance surplus is estimated at around 76.00 - 77.75 billion USD. If global prices for agricultural commodities, iron ore, or oil fall sharply, Brazil could receive less tax revenue and its current account deficit could become worse. The current account deficit is projected at 40.00 to 50.00 billion USD.

Strong External Buffer Protection: Despite the risks from commodity prices, Brazil has a strong external position. Foreign Exchange Reserves to Imports ratio is 123.97%. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) inflows are expected to remain around 80.00 billion USD per year, which is enough to cover the current account deficit.

Global Demand Slowdown

Deceleration in Real GDP Growth: Brazil’s real GDP growth is expected to be moderate at 1.95% in 2026. Growth is expected to remain between 1.80% and 2.00% during 2027-2029.

Global Macro Economic Impact: Weaker global trade or lower demand from Brazil’s main trading partners (US, China, and the EU) could reduce exports. Government consumption is relatively high compared to private consumption, at 30.16%. Because of fiscal limits, the government has less ability to use spending to support the economy during a slowdown.

So What?

These risks are extremely important to financing firm because the more established within a country a bank becomes the harder it is to pull out of a risky country due to instability. These are all things to take into consideration in the long term.
