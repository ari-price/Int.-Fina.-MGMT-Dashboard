Industry Overview

Industry overview: Describe the structural characteristics of the industry (growth rate, cyclicality, capital intensity, regulatory environment) and how they affect the company’s financing strategy.

Growth Rate:

  • Lending in Brazil is still growing, but more slowly. Total credit grew 11.5% in 2024 and 10.3% in 2025. The Central Bank of Brazil (BCB) projects 9.1% in 2026 and 8.8% in 2027
  • Lending to companies (Citi's segment) is growing more slowly than lending to households: 8.2% vs. 9.7% projected for 2026
  • Much of the growth comes from government-program loans (such as farm and housing loans), projected to grow 11.7% in 2026, which are mostly made by state banks, not Citi
  • Why it matters: because loan growth is slowing, Citi's growth depends more on fee-based services (FX, cash management, capital markets) than on making more loans.

Cyclicality

  • Bank profits in Brazil rise and fall with interest rates and the economy. The BCB's policy rate was held at 15% from June 2025 to March 2026, its highest level in almost 20 years, before cuts began in March 2026. In September 2026 it was cut to 13.75%.
  • When the economy slows, more borrowers fall behind on payments. For example, Banco do Brazil's 2025 profit fell 45% because of rising defaults by farm borrowers.
  • Citi has kept its loan book flat in 2025 and plans to stay selective in 2026 while rates remain high.

Capital intensity

  • Banking requires a lot of capital. International rules (Basel III), applied in Brazil by the BCB, require banks to keep a minimum amount of their own capital as a cushion against losses. More lending means the bank needs more capital.
  • Citi Brazil earned R$2.3 billion in 2024 and sent almost all of it to the U.S. parent as a dividend. Management has said it may keep more profit in Brazil to fund growth.

Regulatory environment

  • Banks are regulated by the BCB and the National Monetary Council (CMN).
  • A new accounting rule (CMN Resolution 4,966) took effect in January 2025. It requires banks to set aside money for expected loan losses earlier, which reduced profits across the industry in 2025.
  • The competition regulator (CADE) watches concentration closely. When it approved Itaú's purchase of Citi's consumer business, it noted Citi's small market share and put conditions on Itaú.

How the industry affects Citi's financing strategy

Natural hedge: Citi Brazil funds itself mostly with BRL deposits and lends in BRL. Borrowing in BRL (the local bond in Section 4) matches its BRL assets, so it does not add much new currency risk.
Currency risk on profits: Citi Brazil sends most of its BRL profit to the U.S. parent in dollars [6]. That makes Citi a net receiver of BRL: if the real weakens, those profits are worth fewer dollars. FX forwards (Section 5) can lock in the exchange rate.
High borrowing cost: with the Selic at 13.75%, borrowing in BRL is expensive. A bond with a 4% coupon will sell well below its face value, so its yield (Section 4) will be much higher than 4%.
Falling rates: economists expect the Selic to drop to about 12% in 2027 and 10% by 2029 [16]. This supports using an interest rate swap to switch from a fixed to a floating rate (Section 6). The risk is that rates stay high longer than expected.

Business Risk

Key business risks:

FX & Currency Risk - Citigroup (Citi)

Citigroup faces high exposure to foreign exchange risk as it is currently a net receiver of the Brazilian real, as they sold the consumer branches of their Brazilian franchises. This being said, it is mostly dealing with institution transactions which means the weaker the Real, the larger cuts there are to the USD earnings and USD value of their Brazilian net assets.

This links directly to many financial ratios such as D/E ratio, where a weaker local currency shrinks equity without changes to leverage. This also leads to a risk in the cost of hedging because forward pricing of the BRL would reflect the interest rate gap between it and USD, which can lower returns.

Interest Rate Risk

Brazil currently has one of the highest interest rates in the world with the Selic being at 13.75% as of September 16, after a series of previous cuts. The real interest rate is about 9.5%. This will suppress Net Investment Income on BRL lending, but also increase market value of fixed-rate government bonds, but if the rate goes the other way there are inverse reactions.

  • Depending on the asset and liability mix, the interest rate cuts will either cut NII or boost values of government securities in the market.
  • Local funding costs drop as the Selic is cut, which benefits local debts; benefiting the D/E Ratio

Inflation Rate Risk

The IPCA Inflation rate as of August 2026 was 4.22% in Brazil, with the country having an inflation “ceiling” of 4.5%. However, they are forecasting the possibility of inflation reaching 4.99%. This feeds into three different risks:

  • High inflation keeps interest rates higher which impacts borrowers
  • The inflation rate gap between the USD and BRL feeds back into the FX Risk
  • The efficiency ratio increases due to an increase in operation costs in a Brazilian market
  • Credit losses increase due to inflation strain and high rates

Risk Matrix

Inflation Shocks

Analysis: Although Month-on- month inflation had a contraction (-0.32% in August 2026) and year-on-year CPI stood at 4.22%, inflation is forecasted to reaccelerate to 4.9% by December 2026, which exceeds the Central Bank’s upper target range (4.5%).

FX Pass-Through: With the exchange rate trading around 5.20 BRL/USD and marginal real depreciation, there is a risk of import price pass-through (FX pass-through) into domestic prices, particularly across rigid basket components such as Health (5.84%) and Housing (4.90%).

Monetary Tightening: While the government is expected to slowly lower its main interest rate (the Selic rate) from 13.75% down to 13.50% by December 2026 and 12.00% in 2027, the actual cost of borrowing after inflation will still stay very high (8.5% – 9.5%). This is much higher than the 5.0% balanced rate estimated by the IMF. Keeping interest rates this high will make borrowing expensive, which will slow down the economy's recovery

Political Instability

Government Spending and Debt Problems: The country’s financial health is the biggest risk at home. Total government debt is very high, sitting at 78.9% – 81.5% of the country's total economic output, and it is growing by 5.27% every year. Because the government is projected to spend more than it earns in 2026 (a shortage of -0.42%), its long-term financial future is getting worse.

High Cost of Government Borrowing: Financial markets see the country as risky and are demanding high interest rates to lend it money. Investors want higher returns for locking their money away longer, with rates rising from 13.58% for 1-year bonds up to 14.20% for 10-year bonds, alongside a high insurance cost (203.14 bps) to protect against government default.

Political Risk and Unpredictable Policies: Political tension (0.031 score) shows that arguments in Congress over the budget and upcoming elections are a big problem. These events could force the government to spend even more money, making it break its own rules about responsible spending.

Regulatory Changes

Fiscal Transition and Sectoral Incentives: Changes in taxes and government subsidies may affect costs for different industries and services.

Environmental Framework and ESG Compliance: Brazil has an LSEG Sovereign ESG Global Score of 63.02 and a Sovereign Sustainable GDP Gap of 2.29%. Brazil is facing stricter environmental rules. Requirements to reduce emissions and track supply chains could increase operating costs (OPEX) and create risks, especially for agribusiness and extractive industries.

Commodity Price Fluctuations

Vulnerability to Export Prices: Brazil depends heavily on exports and on the commodity cycle. Its trade balance surplus is estimated at around 76.00 - 77.75 billion USD. If global prices for agricultural commodities, iron ore, or oil fall sharply, Brazil could receive less tax revenue and its current account deficit could become worse. The current account deficit is projected at 40.00 to 50.00 billion USD.

Strong External Buffer Protection: Despite the risks from commodity prices, Brazil has a strong external position. Foreign Exchange Reserves to Imports ratio is 123.97%. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) inflows are expected to remain around 80.00 billion USD per year, which is enough to cover the current account deficit.

Global Demand Slowdown

Deceleration in Real GDP Growth: Brazil’s real GDP growth is expected to be moderate at 1.95% in 2026. Growth is expected to remain between 1.80% and 2.00% during 2027-2029.

Global Macro Economic Impact: Weaker global trade or lower demand from Brazil’s main trading partners (US, China, and the EU) could reduce exports. Government consumption is relatively high compared to private consumption, at 30.16%. Because of fiscal limits, the government has less ability to use spending to support the economy during a slowdown.
